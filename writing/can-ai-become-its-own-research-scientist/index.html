Can AI Become Its Own Research Scientist?

AI can help discover better solutions. Showing that it becomes a better researcher requires a different experiment.

2026-10-18 · Updated 2026-10-24

Finding a better answer and learning how to ask better questions are different achievements.

What would it mean to become a scientist?

An AI system proposes a new algorithm, runs experiments, produces a figure, and writes a paper. The sequence is impressive. But one question would still bother me: what did the system learn from the experiment that changed what it did next?

That question connects my interests in learning, neuroscience, and autonomous AI research. I want to understand how an intelligent system turns experience into better decisions. Scientific research is a demanding setting for this question because the useful next action is rarely obvious. A disappointing result might call for a new hypothesis, a better measurement, or an inspection of the code. Sometimes it should persuade us to stop.

My thesis is that an autonomous researcher must use evidence to improve its decisions, and a self-improving researcher must show that those improvements carry over to new problems under declared resource constraints. A better final artifact is evidence worth examining. It does not, on its own, establish the second claim.

This distinction gives us a practical way to investigate an otherwise enormous question. We can ask which parts of research a system controls, what feedback it receives, and what experiment would demonstrate that its decisions became more useful.

A loop that can change its mind

A useful starting point is a loop:

Question → Hypothesis → Experiment → Observation → Revision → Next question

The arrows matter as much as the boxes. A hypothesis should imply something observable. An experiment should help discriminate between possibilities. An observation should be allowed to weaken the hypothesis that motivated the experiment. Revision should affect a later decision.

Consider a hypothetical optimization study. A system predicts that adding memory will make an algorithm more robust to delayed feedback. It changes several components and obtains a better score. That is a promising candidate, but the proposed explanation remains uncertain. A controlled comparison that changes only the memory mechanism would test the explanation more directly. A further test under different delays would probe its limits.

These are different kinds of progress: improving a score, identifying why it improved, and learning when the mechanism applies. I would want an AI researcher to keep them separate in its records. Otherwise, a successful search can quietly become an unsupported causal story.

This is why I find the closed-loop view useful. It makes research a problem of learning from interventions and allocating attention under uncertainty. It also leaves room for negative results. An experiment that rules out an attractive explanation can improve the next decision even when it produces no better algorithm.

What existing systems actually demonstrate

Existing systems give us several ways to examine this loop, from searching for programs to proposing experiments in a laboratory.

FunSearch starts with a setting where proposals can be checked automatically. A language model generates programs, an evaluator tests them, and useful candidates guide further search. Its reported applications include cap-set constructions in mathematics and online bin packing, where items must be packed as they arrive. The evaluation machinery gives generated ideas somewhere to meet evidence. Google DeepMind’s FunSearch account.

AlphaEvolve broadens this approach to algorithmic tasks including data-center scheduling and components of model training. Its 2025 white paper describes evolutionary program search guided by evaluator feedback. Together, these systems show how a loop can turn generated code into useful algorithmic improvements. Novikov et al., AlphaEvolve.

The AI Scientist-v2 extends the workflow through experiments and manuscript writing. In Sakana AI’s review experiment, humans supplied a broad topic and selected three generated papers for submission. One received scores above an ICLR 2025 workshop’s acceptance threshold. Under the agreed protocol, the papers were withdrawn before publication, and the selected paper received no additional meta-review. The exercise offers a concrete glimpse of automated research reaching human review. Sakana AI’s account.

Co-Scientist brings hypotheses into a different testing environment. Its 2026 Nature paper reports biomedical proposals followed by expert selection and laboratory validation, including in vitro tests of drug-repurposing hypotheses. The evidence comes from the combined work of AI and human scientists, with the reported findings remaining preclinical. Gottweis et al., Accelerating scientific discovery with Co-Scientist.

FrontierScience takes a complementary approach: isolate scientific reasoning tasks and research subtasks in physics, chemistry, and biology, then assess them using defined grading criteria. I see such benchmarks as a way to study ingredients of research while the larger question of choosing and sustaining a research program remains open. OpenAI’s FrontierScience description.

Across these examples, the useful question is where autonomy sits: in generating candidates, executing experiments, interpreting evidence, or choosing an agenda. That gives us a more precise way to discuss progress—and to identify what the next evaluation should test.

The evaluator is part of the scientific claim

My working hypothesis is that, as proposal generation becomes cheaper, the quality of feedback will increasingly constrain useful discovery. Generating a valuable idea remains difficult; deciding what to trust among many plausible ideas presents a challenge of its own.

A trustworthy evaluator can reject an incorrect program quickly. It can also be incomplete. Passing a test suite establishes behavior on those tests; a proof establishes a claim under stated assumptions. Runtime measurements depend on workloads and hardware. A simulator can be internally consistent while omitting the phenomenon we care about.

Even a sensible metric can become misleading through repeated selection. Cawley and Talbot show how optimizing a noisy model-selection criterion can overfit that criterion and bias subsequent performance estimates. The relevance to automated research is straightforward: repeatedly consulting the same evaluation can make a candidate look better without establishing improvement beyond it. Cawley and Talbot, JMLR (2010).

A simple thought experiment makes the issue tangible. Suppose several candidates are equally good, but each measured score contains noise. Choosing the best observed score will tend to select a candidate that benefited from favorable noise. More search creates more opportunities for that selection effect. An independent evaluation is needed to find out what survives.

This also changes how I think about hallucination. An evaluator may catch invalid code, but a fabricated citation or an unjustified biological mechanism can shape which experiments are attempted and how their results are interpreted. Reliability requires checking the evidence chain, including the explanation around the score.

The evaluator therefore belongs inside the scientific argument. We should be able to explain what it measures, what it misses, and why improvement on it bears on the question we claim to answer.

Improving an artifact, improving a research process

There are at least two loops worth separating.

The inner loop searches for a better artifact: a controller, an algorithm, or a candidate hypothesis. It uses feedback to revise proposals within a task. The outer loop changes the procedure that conducts that search: which experiments it selects, how it represents failures, or when it abandons a line of inquiry.

For the outer loop, I would propose a simple operational test:

At a declared budget, does the revised research procedure
produce better independently evaluated outcomes on fresh tasks?

“Fresh” must refer to the claim being made. New random seeds can test robustness within an existing simulator. They do not establish transfer to a different problem family. If we repeatedly redesign a research procedure using the same task suite, that suite becomes development material too.

The budget also needs a definition. Equal numbers of candidate evaluations control one important resource. They do not equalize language-model inference, wall time, or human intervention. Those costs should be recorded separately, and conclusions should match the resources actually controlled. Otherwise, an apparent improvement in research strategy may simply reflect a larger expenditure.

Who makes the revision matters too. To establish autonomous self-improvement, we would need to document how the system proposed a change to its own procedure, which evidence informed it, and whether the change survived an evaluation that neither version could tune against.

This is where I would begin a discussion of recursive self-improvement: with an explicit account of what changes, how the system changes it, and whether the benefit transfers. An upward curve within a single search run answers a narrower question.

Memory should preserve uncertainty

A research system needs more than a growing collection of successful outputs. I would want its memory to preserve the prediction made before an experiment, the exact conditions tested, the observation, and the explanations still compatible with it.

Imagine a record that says, “Adding memory improved performance.” That sentence may hide a simultaneous change in learning rate, one favorable seed, and a failed replication. A better record makes those details available to the next decision. Otherwise, memory can accumulate confidence faster than knowledge.

Research strategy then becomes a choice about which uncertainty is worth resolving. The next experiment might pursue a promising candidate, challenge the current explanation, or check a suspicious measurement. There is no reason to assume that the most elaborate experiment is the most informative one.

These functions can be distributed across agents, but assigning a proposer and a critic does not make their evidence independent. Agreement between two models may reflect shared assumptions. A recomputed result, a counterexample, or a genuinely independent replication gives us a different kind of check. I would evaluate the workflow by the errors it detects and the decisions it improves, rather than by how many scientific roles it imitates.

A small experiment I can make accountable

For my own independent exploration, I started with a synthetic control problem: keeping a simulated system close to a target despite noise and disturbances. The question is whether a proposer that can learn from previous tests chooses useful controller settings more effectively than simple search methods.

Think of a car trying to maintain a chosen speed on a changing road. A controller decides how strongly to respond when the car drifts from its target. A PID controller combines three signals: the current error (proportional), error accumulated over time (integral), and how quickly the error is changing (derivative). Its settings determine the weight given to each signal. In the experiment, “PID-like” means a bounded version of this rule in a simple simulator; the car is an analogy, not the simulated task.

The proposer suggests settings and explains what it expects them to do. Fixed evaluation code then runs the test and records the outcome. The allowed controller family, objective, and evaluation rules stay fixed, and the final test cases remain hidden from the proposer. This makes each prediction easier to compare with what actually happens.

The completed comparison gives random search and sequential local search 20 candidate evaluations each, with a fixed controller as a separate reference. A history-aware model proposer works within the same bounded controller family. After selection on development cases, the frozen winners were evaluated on the registered holdout cases.

The research note reports the result: one live-selected controller had mean holdout cost 0.092063, compared with 0.094983 for the mean of ten random searches and 0.094360 for coordinate search. Both registered primary bootstrap intervals favor that frozen candidate. They do not include variation across independent live searches, because only one history-aware search was run. The full ten-repetition confirmatory comparison remains incomplete.

This is useful evidence about a controller on a small synthetic benchmark. It does not establish that the proposer generally beats simpler methods, that feedback caused the advantage, or that the research procedure improved itself. A no-outcome-feedback run and anti-windup comparisons help frame those questions, but their scope matters too.

The software now has offline checks, bounded budgets, retained checkpoints and saved-response replay verified in two clean local checkouts. Replaying fixed responses reproduces the recorded calculation; it does not reproduce model generation. The original holdout is now inspected and cannot serve as untouched confirmation for further tuning.

This is a first test of evidence-guided search within a fixed problem. It connects proposals, recorded outcomes, and a separately evaluated controller, while leaving the broader value of the research method unresolved. The distinction at the heart of this essay still holds: finding one better artifact and learning a better way to investigate are different claims.

What I would find convincing

The possibility that interests me is a system that becomes better at deciding what evidence it needs. It would recognize when a result is fragile, spend effort distinguishing competing explanations, and carry useful lessons into a new problem without carrying over unwarranted certainty.

A benchmark can test part of that vision. It cannot settle which questions deserve attention or replace the wider work of replication, criticism, and deciding whether a discovery matters. Human judgment remains part of the research process we are trying to understand and improve.

I am optimistic about AI participating more deeply in that process. My optimism rests on making the claims small enough to challenge and the evidence clear enough to revisit.

The experiment I most want to see would distinguish two possibilities: the system found a better answer this time, or it learned a better way to investigate. What result would convince you that the second had happened, and what result would change your mind?
